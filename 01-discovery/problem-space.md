# Problem Space

## Context

Cooking at home is not only a cooking task. Before preparing a meal, people may need to decide what to eat, check what ingredients they have, identify what is missing, and determine whether a recipe fits their time, preferences, and current situation.

Research on meal-kit services suggests that convenience is not limited to receiving ingredients. Users also value having the decision-making and planning work reduced. Studies have identified meal planning, recipe selection, grocery shopping, and preparation as sources of cognitive and practical effort. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

This raises an opportunity to explore a different starting point: instead of asking users to plan a meal first, what if the product starts with the food they already have at home?

## Initial Problem Hypothesis

People may have ingredients available at home but still experience friction when deciding what to cook.

The friction may come from several connected tasks:

- remembering what is currently available
- checking ingredients manually
- finding recipes that match those ingredients
- determining which missing ingredients are actually necessary
- deciding whether a recipe fits the current meal context
- repeatedly making the same meal-planning decision

The problem may therefore be less about a lack of recipes and more about the effort required to turn available ingredients into a realistic meal decision.

## Opportunity

FridgeMate explores whether AI can reduce the effort between:

**Physical kitchen → Ingredient inventory → Meal decision**

Instead of requiring users to manually enter their ingredients before receiving recommendations, FridgeMate could use AI to create a starting inventory from fridge photos.

The AI would assist with identifying ingredients, while the user remains responsible for confirming and correcting the inventory.

## Initial Hypotheses

These hypotheses are not yet validated:

1. People who cook at home may experience recurring friction when deciding what to cook.

2. Existing ingredients may not automatically translate into a clear meal decision.

3. Manually maintaining a digital ingredient inventory may create enough friction to discourage regular use.

4. Starting the inventory process with photos could reduce the effort required to describe what is available.

5. Recipe recommendations become more useful when they reflect the user's actual available ingredients rather than generic recipe discovery.

6. Meal context, preferences, and cuisine may influence whether a recommendation feels relevant.

7. Users may prefer assistance with the decision rather than having an AI make the decision entirely for them.

## Key Research Question

> Can FridgeMate reduce the effort required to go from "I have ingredients at home" to "I know what I can cook"?

## What We Need to Validate

The most important unknowns are:

- Do people actually experience this problem frequently?
- What do they currently do when they do not know what to cook?
- How much effort do they spend checking ingredients and searching for recipes?
- Do they already use recipe, meal-planning, or ingredient-inventory products?
- Would taking photos of their fridge feel easier than manually entering ingredients?
- How much correction would users expect to make after AI detection?
- What makes a recipe recommendation feel realistic and relevant?
- Would users return to the product after the initial scan?

These questions will guide the next stage of research.
